# Benchmarking methods for inferring single-cell transcription factor activity using large-scale perturbation sequencing data

Yuehui Zhu, Dongmei Han, Zhen Wang

DOI: 10.1093/bib/bbag513

## Abstract

AbstractTranscription factor activity (TFA) is determined not solely by the expression level of the transcription factor (TF) gene itself, but is also modulated by a series of post-transcriptional regulatory processes. Although numerous computational methods have been developed to infer TFA from single-cell transcriptomic data by constructing gene regulatory networks (GRNs), a systematic and unified evaluation of these methods using high-quality experimental data remains lacking in the field. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of eight mainstream TFA inference methods spanning three categories—prior GRN-based, de novo GRN-based, and integrated GRN-based approaches—using large-scale, high-quality single-cell perturbation sequencing (Perturb-seq) datasets. Our results demonstrate that metaTF, which employs an integrated GRN, achieves the best performance across multiple metrics, including TF coverage, predictive accuracy for perturbed cells, and accuracy for perturbed TFs. Among de novo GRN-based methods, pySCENIC exhibits predictive accuracy second only to metaTF but with lower TF coverage; meanwhile, decoupleR, a prior GRN-based method, ranks highly across all evaluated metrics. Further investigation reveals that the enrichment of reconstructed regulons within differentially expressed genes, the selection of prior GRNs and TFA scoring algorithms, and the perturbation types of target TFs are all critical factors influencing the accuracy of TFA inference. This study provides practical recommendations for the application and development of TFA inference methods.

## Introduction

Transcription factors (TFs) are key regulators of gene expression, driving cell fate determination and functional maintenance. Transcription factor activity (TFA) serves as a quantitative measure of the extent to which a specific TF drives the expression of its downstream target genes within a given cell. The execution of TF function is a highly complex and dynamic process that depends not only on its own mRNA transcription level, but is also influenced by a series of post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms, including translation efficiency, post-translational modifications, subcellular localization, and protein–protein interactions, all of which are tightly regulated by cellular signaling pathways [1, 2].

As the transcriptional level of TF genes alone cannot accurately reflect their true biological activity, current strategies for inferring TFA from transcriptome data primarily rely on the construction of gene regulatory networks (GRNs) [3]. A GRN systematically describes the regulatory relationships between TFs and their target genes, serving as the core framework for understanding gene expression regulatory mechanisms. The fundamental unit of a GRN is the regulon, defined as the set of regulatory relationships between a specific TF and its target genes. Existing computational methods infer TFA quantitatively by evaluating the aggregate expression levels of target genes within a given regulon [4–6]. Although this concept was initially developed for bulk RNA-seq data analysis and successfully applied in cancer research [7–9], the widespread adoption of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology has brought transformative advances to this field [3, 10]. The massive cell numbers and high degree of intercellular heterogeneity provided by scRNA-seq establish a substantially more powerful data foundation for constructing high-resolution GRNs and accurately inferring TFA at the single-cell level.

For scRNA-seq data, a large number of TFA inference methods have been developed. Based on differences in GRN construction strategies, these methods can be broadly classified into three categories (Table 1). The first category comprises prior GRN-based methods, with decoupleR [11] being a representative tool. The prior GRN recommended by decoupleR is CollecTRI [12], a high-confidence regulatory network compiled from 12 database sources including DorothEA [13] and supported by experimental evidence. The second category consists of data-driven, de novo GRN construction methods, represented by tools such as SCENIC/pySCENIC [14, 15], hdWGCNA [16, 17], PISCES [18, 19], and scMINER [20]. Among these, SCENIC/pySCENIC and hdWGCNA construct GRNs based on co-expression relationships between TFs and target genes using machine learning approaches (e.g. XGBoost), whereas PISCES and scMINER employ information-theoretic algorithms for GRN construction [21, 22]. The third category encompasses integrative methods, represented by tools such as TIGER [23], metaTF [24], and scRegulate [25]. These methods adopt machine learning or information-theoretic frameworks to integrate a prior GRN with a GRN inferred from scRNA-seq data, aiming to achieve more accurate TFA inference by leveraging prior knowledge. Notably, the above classification reflects preferences in GRN information sources rather than absolute distinctions; for instance, pySCENIC and hdWGCNA also utilize transcription factor binding site (TFBS) information from promoter sequences to filter TF–target gene relationships.

Despite the widespread application of TFA inference in scRNA-seq data analysis, systematic evaluation of the performance of different methods remains lacking. This is primarily attributed to the fact that the true TFA within cells is often unknown and difficult to measure directly [3]. Some prior GRN-based methods, such as decoupleR leveraging CollecTRI [12], have been evaluated using bulk-level TF knockout data, but this approach is not applicable to tools that require de novo GRN construction from single-cell data. Single-cell perturbation sequencing (Perturb-seq) offers a high-throughput solution to this challenge [26]. In Perturb-seq, single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting genes of interest and their corresponding guide barcodes (GBCs) are simultaneously delivered into individual cells via CRISPR lentiviral vectors. Following the CRISPR-mediated genetic perturbation, scRNA-seq captures the GBC to identify the sgRNA received by each cell, thereby determining the genetically perturbed gene. In a previous study, Holland CH et al. benchmarked three prior GRN-based scoring methods and SCENIC using single-gene perturbation data of 22 TFs from two Perturb-seq datasets [10]. Perturb-seq data have also been employed for benchmarking during the development of tools such as decoupleR, scMINER, and metaTF; however, these evaluations were limited to specific datasets and comparisons among a small number of methods [11, 20, 24]. Therefore, there remains an urgent need for a systematic benchmarking study encompassing a broader range of Perturb-seq datasets, a richer repertoire of TF perturbation events, and a more comprehensive coverage of TFA inference method categories.

In this study, we conducted a systematic and unified evaluation of eight representative tools across three categories of TFA inference methods using large-scale Perturb-seq data (Fig. 1). To construct a comprehensive and high-quality benchmark dataset, we implemented stringent quality control (QC) procedures on the collected Perturb-seq datasets. Building upon standard scRNA-seq QC, we additionally excluded false-positive cells potentially arising from perturbation escape and removed ineffective perturbation groups with weak perturbation effects. Ultimately, we obtained 17 high-quality Perturb-seq datasets encompassing 174 single-gene perturbations targeting 135 independent TFs. We subsequently evaluated the performance of TFA inference methods across multiple dimensions, including TF coverage, the accuracy of perturbed cell identification, the accuracy of perturbed TF identification, and computational efficiency. The evaluation results demonstrate that metaTF, which utilizes an integrated network, exhibits excellent performance across all aforementioned metrics, and its constructed regulons also show the highest enrichment among differentially expressed genes (DEGs) following perturbation. Furthermore, we explored the factors influencing the performance of TFA inference methods and found that GRN construction strategies, the choice of prior GRNs and TFA scoring algorithms, and the perturbation modalities of target TFs all exert significant effects on the accuracy of TFA inference methods.

Notably, although various foundation models have been applied to GRN construction tasks [27, 28], few published studies have leveraged these foundation models for TFA inference to date, meaning such strategies have not yet become routinely adopted analytical practices in this field. Furthermore, whether current foundation models can bring improvements to perturbation effect prediction remains an open question within the community [29, 30]. Therefore, in this benchmark, we excluded methods based on foundation models.


## Material and methods


### Perturb-seq datasets

We initially screened 122 Perturb-seq datasets collected from the PerturBase database (queried in September 2025) [31] using the following criteria: “Organism” = “Homo sapiens;” “Modality” = “RNA;” “Model” = “Cell line;” “Perturbation Impact” = “Activation” or “Inhibition;” and “QC” = “Pass,” yielding 40 Perturb-seq datasets. For each dataset, we retained cells with perturbation labels designated as control or TF. Conventional QC was performed on the raw gene expression count matrices using scanpy (version 1.11.4) [32], retaining cells with >500 detected genes and <10% mitochondrial gene expression, and genes expressed in at least three cells. We further removed confounding effects using pertpy (version 1.0.2) [33].The mixscape algorithm [34] (with 4000 highly variable genes) was applied to identify and exclude false-positive cells labeled as perturbed but exhibiting perturbation escape. The E-test algorithm [35] (1000 permutations, using "X_pca" dimensions, significance level of 0.05) was employed to identify and exclude perturbation groups with weak effects indistinguishable from the control group, i.e. ineffective perturbations. Effective perturbation groups were required to contain >30 cells. On this basis, for CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) perturbations, we selected those with decreased target TF expression relative to the control group (log2-fold change (FC) < 0); for CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) or open reading frame (ORF) overexpression perturbations, we selected those with increased target TF expression relative to the control group (log2-FC > 0). Ultimately, we obtained 17 Perturb-seq datasets [26, 36–45], encompassing 174 perturbations of 135 unique TFs, constituting a comprehensive and high-quality benchmark dataset.


### TF annotations

Human TFs were identified based on the Gene Ontology (GO) [46, 47] term GO:0003700 (Name: DNA-binding transcription factor activity), with the criteria “Organism” = “Homo sapiens” and “Type” = “protein.” After deduplication, 1423 TFs were obtained. TF DNA-binding domain (DBD) class annotations were retrieved from the TFClass database [48].


### TFA inference methods

We systematically compared eight TFA inference methods (Table 1), including the prior GRN-based method (decoupleR); de novo GRN-based methods (pySCENIC, PISCES, hdWGCNA, and scMINER); and integrative GRN-based methods (TIGER, metaTF, and scRegulate). In our benchmark pipeline, all methods were supplied with identical initial input matrices and unified preprocessing workflows at the outset; nevertheless, several approaches incorporated additional customized steps including cell QC, normalization, and feature selection (Supplementary Table S1).


### decoupleR

decoupleR [11] is available in both Python and R implementations; we employed the Python version (version 2.1.1). decoupleR infers biological activity based on prior knowledge and integrates 11 TFA scoring algorithms (ULM [11], AUCell [14], VIPER [4], GSEA [49], GSVA [50], MDT [11], MLM [11], ORA [51], UDT [11], WAGGR [11], and Zscore [52]), with the capability to generate a Consensus score. Unless otherwise specified, decoupleR refers to the configuration using the CollecTRI [12] prior GRN with the Consensus scoring algorithm.


### pySCENIC

pySCENIC (version 0.12.1) [15] is a Python package representing an efficient implementation of the R-based SCENIC [14]. The pySCENIC analytical pipeline comprises three principal steps: first, GRNBoost2 [53] is employed to infer co-expression modules between TFs and potential target genes from scRNA-seq data. Second, RcisTarget is used to perform motif enrichment analysis and target gene filtering based on TF binding motif rankings within the transcription start site region of target genes (the motif database: H. sapiens - hg38 - refseq_r80 – mc v10 clust, 500bp_up_100bp_down and 10kbp_up_10kbp_down files; Motif2TF annotations: motifs-v10nr_clust-nr version). Third, AUCell [15] is applied to the de novo constructed GRNs and scRNA-seq data to derive TFA scores. Additionally, we conducted a longitudinal comparison using the deprecated legacy version of motif rankings (motif database: H. sapiens - hg38 - refseq_r80 – v9 databases, 500bp_up_100bp_down and 10kbp_up_10kbp_down files; Motif2TF annotations: motifs-v9-nr version).


### PISCES

PISCES (version 0.0.0.9000) [19] is an R package with an analytical pipeline comprising the following steps: first, scRNA-seq data are converted into metacell expression profiles to alleviate sparsity; second, ARACNe3 (the latest implementation of the algorithm for the reconstruction of accurate cellular networks) [18], based on mutual information (MI), is employed to infer potential regulons and prune them to obtain a GRN; finally, NaRnEA (nonparametric analytical rank-based enrichment analysis) [54] is used to score TFA.


### hdWGCNA

hdWGCNA (version 0.4.8) [16] is an R package primarily designed for single-cell gene co-expression network construction. Its TFA inference pipeline encompasses the following steps: first, metacells are constructed and co-expression analysis is performed based on inter-gene expression correlations; second, potential regulons are obtained through promoter TFBS prediction using the JASPAR2024 database [55], and regulons are subsequently filtered based on regulatory importance using XGBoost [56]; finally, Ucell [57] is applied to the constructed GRNs for TFA scoring.


### scMINER

scMINER (version 1.1.0) [20] is an R package that employs MI-based strategies for scRNA-seq data analysis. Following cell clustering, scMINER uses SJARACNe (an improved implementation of ARACNe) [58] for GRN inference, and finally derives TFA as the mean expression of the target genes within each regulon.


### TIGER

TIGER (version 0.0.0.9000) [23] is an R package that decomposes the expression matrix into a GRN matrix (gene × TF) and a TFA matrix (TF × cell). TIGER can leverage a prior GRN to simultaneously estimate the posterior distributions of transcriptional regulatory weights and TFA through Bayesian inference. The default prior database for TIGER is DoRothEA [13]. Due to server memory constraints, TIGER was unable to estimate TFA for the largest benchmark dataset (GSE133344).


### metaTF

metaTF (version 0.1.9) is an R package [24] that employs integrative GRNs for TFA inference. First, metaTF provides a distinctive prior GRN database compiled from four sources: manually curated datasets, ChIP-seq binding datasets, TF knockout experiments, and tissue-specific transcriptional regulatory networks. Concurrently, metaTF utilizes PUIC (percentage of unique information contribution) [59], an information-theoretic algorithm, for GRN construction; PUIC estimates the significance of TF–target gene relationships by quantifying the unique contribution of MI among each gene triplet. The two GRNs are then weighted, aggregated, and pruned to obtain an integrative GRN, following which VIPER [4] is applied for TFA scoring.


### scRegulate

scRegulate (version 1.0.1) [25] is a Python package that trains a variational autoencoder (VAE) model constructed with multilayer perceptrons comprising linear layers and ReLU activation functions [60]. The encoder maps the expression matrix to a latent space, while the decoder first transforms latent variables into a TFA matrix, and subsequently reconstructs the expression matrix through a linear layer whose weight matrix encodes the GRN. Both the TFA matrix and the weight matrix are initialized based on prior information and are progressively updated during training as iterations increase. The default prior GRN for scRegulate is CollecTRI [12].


### Evaluation metrics

TF coverage: the proportion of 174 TF perturbations for which TFA was predicted by each TFA inference method.

AUROC and AUPRC: for each TF perturbation, the perturbed cells of that TF served as the positive set and control cells as the negative set, with the predicted TFA from each method used as the prediction score to calculate the AUROC and AUPRC.

Early precision (EP): for each TF perturbation, EP is defined as the proportion of true positive cells for that TF among the top k cells ranked by the predicted TFA from each method, where k equals the actual number of true positive cells for that TF.

Normalized rank (NRank): for each perturbed cell, z-scores of all predicted TFAs relative to the Control group were computed and ranked globally. NRank is defined as the ratio of the rank of the actually perturbed TF to the total number of TFs included in the prediction. A lower NRank value indicates superior inference performance for the perturbed TF.

We compared the AUROC and NRank across methods using the paired sample t-test, and the AUPRC and EP using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. P-values from all multiple hypothesis tests were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Effect sizes were reported as the mean difference for the t-test, and the Hodges-Lehmann estimate (pseudo-median) for the Wilcoxon test. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for effect sizes were calculated using bootstrap resampling. The baseline for these metrics was established by assigning TFA values drawn from a uniform distribution over (0.0, 1.0) (seed = 6).


### Leave-one-dataset-out analysis

To mitigate the potential confounding effect arising from disproportionately high proportions of TF perturbations in individual datasets, we conducted 17 independent statistical analyses, each leaving out one of the 17 benchmark datasets.

### Prior GRN databases

CollecTRI was obtained via the Python package decoupler (version 2.1.2) [11] using “dc.op.collectri(organism = ‘human’).” DoRothEA was retrieved through the R package dorothea (version 1.14.1) [13], including “dorothea_hs” (human normal tissues) and “dorothea_hs_pancancer” (human cancer tissues). TFLink [61] was downloaded with the “H. sapiens small-scale interaction GMT gene name” version. ChIP-Atlas [62] was downloaded from the Target Genes module of ChIP-Atlas 3.0, with “Organism” = “H. sapiens (hg38)” and “Distance from TSS” = “±1 k.” For each TF, target genes were filtered by MACS2 score ≥ 100, and the top 500 target genes ranked by score were retained.


### Scoring algorithms

To evaluate the impact of scoring algorithms, we replaced the default scoring functions of other TFA inference methods with the scoring algorithms provided by decoupleR. We additionally implemented the TFA scoring algorithm Ucell [63], which calculates, for each cell, the enrichment scores of positive regulatory target genes and negative regulatory target genes for each regulon based on gene expression rankings, and derives normalized Ucell scores ([0.0, 1.0]).


### Sequencing quality of perturbed cells

We evaluated the impact of sequencing quality in perturbed cells on prediction performance, including the perturbed cell number, perturbed cell fraction, median UMI counts, and median dropout rates across perturbed cells. These metrics were measured for each TF perturbation based on per-cell perturbation labels in the Perturb-seq datasets. Dropout rates for individual cells were inferred using the method provided by BayesImpute [64].

### Computer resource consumption

All runtime and memory profiling experiments were conducted on an Ubuntu 20.04.6 LTS system. The “time” command (GNU bash, version 5.0.17(1)-release) was used to record the runtime and memory resource consumption of each TFA inference method across varying dataset scales. The server was equipped with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6248R CPU @ 3.00 GHz with 96 logical cores; each method was restricted to single-core execution.


## Result


### Benchmarking datasets and TFA inference methods

We constructed a comprehensive, high-quality benchmarking dataset based on the PerturBase database [31]. The dataset comprises 17 rigorously curated human cell line Perturb-seq datasets, encompassing 174 single-gene perturbations targeting 135 independent TFs (Supplementary Table S2). Given the inherent limitations of experimental techniques such as off-target effects associated with CRISPR technology [65], we implemented additional QC following the standard scRNA-seq preprocessing [32]. Specifically, we employed the mixscape algorithm to identify and exclude false-positive cells labeled as perturbed groups that had actually undergone perturbation escape, and utilized the E-test algorithm to detect and remove ineffective perturbation groups with weak perturbation effects [34, 35].

Despite the included TFs accounting for only 9.5% (135/1423) of the total human TF repertoire, these TFs span 18 classes based on the DBD classification of TFClass [48], providing adequate representation of the major types of human TFs (Fig. 2A). The 174 TF perturbations serve as the fundamental benchmarking units in our study, comprising 102 activating perturbations generated by CRISPRa and ORF overexpression technologies, alongside 72 repressive perturbations produced by CRISPRi and CRISPR knockout (CRISPR-KO) technologies (Fig. 2B). Owing to the diverse origins of these datasets from different published studies, a variety exists across datasets in terms of perturbation numbers and cell numbers. The largest dataset, GSE133344, encompasses 41 TF-activating perturbations and 28 732 cells (Fig. 2B and C), whereas the number of cells per perturbed TF ranges from 33 to 1708 (Supplementary Fig. S1). Collectively, our dataset encompasses a broad spectrum of TF types, perturbation modalities, and cell numbers, thereby providing a suitable benchmark reference for the systematic evaluation of TFA inference method performance.

(B) Distribution of the number and type of 174 TF perturbations across 17 benchmark datasets. (C) Distribution of the number of perturbed and control cells across 17 benchmark datasets.

We systematically collected eight recently published TFA inference tools designed for scRNA-seq data (Table 1). Although these methods differ in their specific implementations, they all adhere to the core principle of first constructing GRNs and subsequently evaluating TFA based on the constructed networks. According to the primary source of GRN information, we classified these TFA inference methods into three major categories: prior GRN-based methods, de novo GRN-based methods, and integrative methods combining both approaches. Furthermore, given that some tools allow flexible configuration of prior GRN databases or TFA scoring algorithms, yielding method variants, we initially evaluated each tool using its default or recommended configuration, followed by an assessment of the impact of altering these settings. Other customized implementation choices, including input requirements, additional QC, normalization, and feature selection, were kept at their default settings (Supplementary Table S1).


### Performance of TFA inference methods

We first examined the TF coverage of each TFA inference method, defined as the proportion of perturbed TFs covered by its constructed GRNs (Fig. 3A). In this evaluation, only TIGER was limited by computational resource constraints and was consequently evaluated on merely 16 Perturb-seq datasets comprising 133 TF perturbations, whereas the other seven methods were evaluated on 17 Perturb-seq datasets comprising 174 TF perturbations. The results showed that metaTF achieved the highest coverage (99.4%), followed by scMINER, PISCES, and decoupleR, all of which attained coverage rates of ~90%, whereas pySCENIC exhibited the poorest coverage (47.7%). Running pySCENIC with an older version of the TF annotation file could improve its coverage to 56.9%, but this still remained lower than that of the other methods (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Subsequently, among the TFs covered by each method, we evaluated the accuracy of perturbed cell prediction. For this assessment, control group cells served as the negative set, while cells from specific TF perturbation groups served as the positive set. We employed two threshold-independent evaluation metrics: AUROC (Fig. 3B) and AUPRC (Fig. 3C). AUROC provides a uniform random baseline of 0.5, facilitating cross-dataset comparisons, whereas AUPRC is commonly used in scenarios with imbalanced positive and negative samples, although its random baseline depends on the class ratio. We found that metaTF, based on an integrated GRN, achieved optimal performance across both metrics (mean AUROC = 0.681, median AUPRC = 0.179) and was significantly superior to other methods (statistical tests and exact P-values are provided in Supplementary Table S3). The second-best performer was pySCENIC, which constructs GRNs de novo (mean AUROC = 0.645, median AUPRC = 0.146), while decoupleR, a prior GRN-based method, also attained a top-ranking accuracy (mean AUROC = 0.597, median AUPRC = 0.102).

To address the uneven distribution of TF perturbations across the 17 benchmark datasets, we conducted a leave-one-dataset-out analysis. The results remained consistent with those obtained from the full dataset, with metaTF consistently exhibiting a significant advantage over other methods as measured by AUROC (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Considering that researchers investigating TFA often focus on cell populations with highly active TFs, we further compared these methods using EP (Supplementary Fig. S4). The results showed that only metaTF, pySCENIC, and hdWGCNA exhibited significant differences from the random baseline.

Next, we evaluated the prediction accuracy of each method for the perturbed TF within perturbed cells. Since each perturbed cell corresponds to a single TF perturbation, we employed NRank of the perturbed TF’s TFA relative to the control group as the evaluation metric, with lower values indicating better performance (Fig. 3D). We found that the top three methods by NRank were metaTF (mean NRank = 0.327), pySCENIC (mean NRank = 0.352), and decoupleR (mean NRank = 0.413), indicating that these methods exhibit good consistency between perturbed cell identification and perturbed TF identification.

Finally, we examined the Pearson correlations of prediction accuracy across different TF perturbations among these methods (Fig. 3E). The results revealed a high correlation between metaTF and pySCENIC (correlation = 0.80), indicating substantial consistency in their predictive performance across different TFs. In contrast, the correlation between decoupleR and metaTF was comparatively lower (correlation = 0.29), suggesting that these two methods exhibit complementary strengths and weaknesses in predicting the effects of different TF perturbations.

Taken together, in the benchmarking of these methods using default settings, metaTF achieved optimal performance in both TF coverage and prediction accuracy. pySCENIC ranked second in prediction accuracy and exhibited highly correlated performance to that of metaTF, but suffered from insufficient TF coverage. decoupleR, which relies entirely on prior GRNs, maintained a top-ranking position across all evaluated metrics and demonstrated distinct prediction specificity for individual TFs compared with metaTF.


### Regulon enrichment analysis

The inference of TFA is fundamentally dependent on specific GRN construction strategies. As the basic unit of a GRN, the reliability and accuracy of regulons are critical for all TFA inference methods. Although obtaining precise target gene sets is inherently challenging, DEGs between TF perturbation and control groups in Perturb-seq experiments naturally encompass the downstream target genes of the perturbed TF. Therefore, we can evaluate the regulons constructed by each method through their enrichment within these DEGs.

Taking TF IRF1 as an example, IRF1 is one of the major regulators of the innate immune system, and through its N-terminal DBD, it can bind to and activate the promoters of type I interferon genes [66, 67]. Among the DEGs between the IRF1 perturbation and control groups in the GSE146194 (exp8) dataset, we identified well-established positive regulatory target genes of IRF1, including IFI35, IFIT3, PSMB9, TAP1, TAP2, and TAPBP, which span the type I interferon pathway and MHC class I/II antigen presentation pathways [68–73] (Fig. 4A). Analysis revealed that the IRF1 regulon constructed by metaTF exhibited significantly greater enrichment within these DEGs (adjusted P = 1.05e-11, hypergeometric test) compared with those constructed by pySCENIC (adjusted P = 5.62e-08) and decoupleR (adjusted P = 3.46e-06), consistent with its superior predictive AUROC performance (Fig. 4B).

We performed enrichment analysis on all regulons and found that metaTF had the largest number of regulons passing the hypergeometric test (adjusted P < .05) and the highest odds ratio (Supplementary Fig. S5). To exclude the influence of other factors, we examined the relationship between regulon enrichment and AUROC within the same method. By grouping regulons based on whether they passed the hypergeometric test, we found that all three representative methods—metaTF, pySCENIC, and decoupleR—exhibited significantly higher AUROC for regulons that passed the test compared with those that failed (Fig. 4C; statistical tests and exact P-values are provided in Supplementary Table S4). These results demonstrate that the enrichment of reconstructed regulons within DEGs is a critical factor influencing prediction accuracy.


### Impact of prior GRNs

Next, we used AUROC as the primary metric to evaluate the impact of adjusting default configurations on the TFA prediction accuracy of each method. We first focused on metaTF, which exhibited the highest prediction accuracy. As this method integrates both a prior GRN (in-house database) and a de novo constructed GRN (using PUIC), we separately assessed the contributions of these two types of GRNs to AUROC. The results demonstrated that using either the prior GRNs or the de novo constructed GRNs alone yielded significantly lower prediction accuracy compared with the fully integrated model, indicating that both sources of information are indispensable for the superior performance of metaTF (Fig. 5A; statistical tests and exact P-values are provided in Supplementary Table S5).

For decoupleR, which relies exclusively on prior GRNs, the recommended prior database is CollecTRI, an upgraded version of the DoRothEA database developed by the same team [12, 13]. To investigate the impact of prior GRNs on decoupleR, we replaced CollecTRI with two independently developed databases: TFLink [61] and ChIP-Atlas [62]. TFLink is compiled from multiple data sources, from which we selected only regulatory relationships supported by small-scale experimental evidence; ChIP-Atlas, conversely, contains exclusively interactions supported by large-scale ChIP-seq experiments. Although these three databases share a relatively large number of TFs, their common interaction pairs are extremely limited (Supplementary Fig. S6). The benchmarking results showed that the AUROC based on CollecTRI was significantly higher than that based on TFLink, validating the rationale for decoupleR’s default prior GRN configuration (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Fig. S7; statistical tests and exact P-values are provided in Supplementary Table S5).

We also analyzed other methods for which the prior GRNs could be substituted. For scRegulate, compared with CollecTRI (default) or TFLink, a significant improvement in AUROC was observed when ChIP-Atlas was adopted as the prior GRN (Fig. 5C; statistical tests and exact P-values are provided in Supplementary Table S5). For TIGER, no significant differences in AUROC performance were detected among the use of DoRothEA (default), ChIP-Atlas, or CollecTRI as the prior GRN (Fig. 5D; statistical tests and exact P-values are provided in Supplementary Table S5). These findings indicate that the optimal prior GRN may differ across different methods.


### Impact of scoring algorithms

Upon determining the regulon, scoring algorithms aggregate the expression levels of target genes into a TFA score. In principle, any single-cell gene set scoring algorithm can serve this purpose; however, existing scoring algorithms exhibit extensive differences in expression data normalization and aggregation strategies [74]. The decoupleR package systematically integrates 12 TFA scoring algorithms. Using CollecTRI as the prior GRN, we conducted a comprehensive comparison of all TFA scoring algorithms implemented in decoupleR, along with another commonly used method, Ucell [57, 63]. We found that the single TFA scoring algorithm ULM achieved AUROC performance comparable to that of Consensus and was significantly superior to or comparable with other TFA scoring algorithms (Fig. 6A; statistical tests and exact P-values are provided in Supplementary Table S6). This finding is consistent with the original report on decoupleR [11].

Other TFA inference methods typically incorporate only a single built-in TFA scoring algorithm. We therefore analyzed methods that allow substitution of TFA scoring algorithms. The results showed that replacing the default AUCell in pySCENIC and the default VIPER in metaTF with ULM led to significant improvements in predictive AUROC for both methods (Fig. 6B and C; statistical tests and exact P-values are provided in Supplementary Table S6). This demonstrates that ULM is not only applicable as a TFA scoring algorithm for prior GRN-based methods, but can also be effectively generalized to TFA inference methods utilizing other GRN types.


### Impact of perturbation methods and TF DBD classes

We next investigated the influence of non-model factors on TFA prediction performance. First, we explored the effect of TF perturbation modalities for metaTF, pySCENIC, and decoupleR. We found that both metaTF and pySCENIC exhibited significantly superior AUROC for TF activation compared with inhibition, whereas decoupleR showed no such effect (Fig. 7A; statistical tests and exact P-values are provided in Supplementary Table S7). Furthermore, metaTF demonstrated significantly better prediction for TFs affected by CRISPRa than for those affected by ORF overexpression or CRISPRi; pySCENIC showed significantly better prediction for TFs affected by CRISPRa and ORF overexpression than for those affected by CRISPRi; again, decoupleR exhibited no such effect (Fig. 7B; statistical tests and exact P-values are provided in Supplementary Table S7). This may be attributable to the fact that de novo GRN-based TFA inference methods leverage gene expression information, thereby displaying preferential sensitivity toward specific perturbation modalities, whereas prior GRN-based decoupleR remains more robust in this regard.

To evaluate the potential effects of sequencing quality on method performance, we explored the perturbed cell number, perturbed cell fraction, median UMI counts, and median dropout rates among perturbed cells. The results showed that the AUROC values of both metaTF and pySCENIC were significantly correlated with the perturbed cell number and median dropout rates, whereas decoupleR showed no significant correlations with any of these factors (Supplementary Table S8). However, after controlling for the effect of perturbation methods using a multiple linear regression model, the additional contributions of perturbed cell number and median dropout rates to the variance in AUROC for metaTF, pySCENIC, and decoupleR were all non-significant (Supplementary Table S9).

Thereafter, we investigated the effect of TF DBD classes [48] on prediction performance for each of the three tools. In this analysis, we again controlled for the effect of perturbation methods using a multiple linear regression model. Overall, this linear model significantly explained the variance in AUROC for metaTF and pySCENIC (metaTF: adjusted R2 = 0.351, P = 2.56e-11; pySCENIC: adjusted R2 = 0.526, P = 3.08e-09, F-test), but not for decoupleR (adjusted R2 = 0.028, P = .209, F-test). We further conducted ANOVA to quantify the contribution of individual factors for the former two methods. For metaTF, perturbation methods accounted for 23.71% of the variance (P = 3.57e-10), whereas TF DBD classes contributed 12.54% (P = 4.05e-03, F-test). For pySCENIC, perturbation methods and TF DBD classes explained 29.93% (P = 5.16e-07) and 23.56% of the variance (P = 3.07e-04, F-test), respectively. Specifically, DBD classes of other C4 zinc finger-type factors (∆AUROC = 0.163, adjusted P = 6.54e-03) and tryptophan cluster factors (∆AUROC = 0.143, adjusted P = 6.06e-03, two-tailed t-test) exhibited significantly superior performance compared with the overall average. Collectively, these results indicate that metaTF and pySCENIC exhibit varying degrees of TF DBD class bias, whereas decoupleR remains relatively robust to this factor (Fig. 7C; statistical tests and exact P-values are provided in Supplementary Table S10).


### Analysis of computational efficiency and resource consumption

Finally, we comprehensively assessed the computational efficiency and resource consumption of each TFA inference method across varying data scales. Under single-core constraints, we recorded the runtime and peak memory usage for each method on datasets comprising 3000 cells, 6000 cells, and 9000 cells. Most methods were executed with default configurations; for decoupleR, we additionally evaluated both ULM and Consensus scoring modes. The results indicated that the runtime of most methods increased linearly with dataset size. Notably, decoupleR (ULM) exhibited the shortest runtime, whereas hdWGCNA, pySCENIC, and scRegulate demonstrated moderate performance. By contrast, PISCES, scMINER, TIGER, metaTF, and decoupleR (Consensus) required substantially longer runtimes than the other methods (Fig. 8A). Regarding memory usage, all methods exhibited increased peak memory consumption as the dataset size expanded. decoupleR (ULM) and scRegulate demonstrated relatively modest peak memory requirements, whereas metaTF and TIGER exhibited substantially higher peak memory usage than the other methods (Fig. 8B).

Notably, although metaTF achieved the best predictive performance, its runtime and peak RAM usage were tens to hundreds of times higher than those of pySCENIC, which ranked second. For users considering decoupleR, given that ULM and Consensus achieved comparable prediction accuracy while ULM demonstrated significant advantages in both computational speed and memory consumption, we recommend ULM as the preferred scoring algorithm (Fig. 8C and D).


## Discussion

We conducted a comprehensive benchmark of eight existing TFA inference methods for scRNA-seq data using large-scale, high-quality Perturb-seq datasets. We systematically compared these methods across TF coverage, prediction accuracy for perturbed cells, and prediction accuracy for perturbed TFs. Furthermore, we evaluated the impact of regulon enrichment, prior GRNs and scoring algorithm selection, as well as other non-model factors, including perturbation modality and TF DBD class preference, on prediction accuracy.

We categorized TFA inference methods into three major classes based on their GRN construction strategies: prior GRN-based, de novo GRN-based, and integrative GRN-based approaches. Knowledge-driven prior GRNs typically compile TF–target gene relationships supported by experimental evidence, offering high confidence but lacking tissue-type and cell-type specificity. In contrast, data-driven de novo GRNs are constructed primarily from co-expression relationships within scRNA-seq data, conferring strong tissue and cell-type specificity, yet they may lack independent experimental validation. In recent years, integrative approaches that combine static prior knowledge with dynamic data features have emerged, aiming to achieve complementary advantages from both information sources.

We found that among all evaluated methods, metaTF, which employs an integrative GRN, demonstrated significant advantages across key metrics including TF coverage, perturbed cell prediction, and perturbed TF prediction. metaTF is a novel approach that first assembles TF–target interactions from diverse sources—including curated databases, ChIP-seq data, and perturbation experiments (e.g. TF knockout/knockdown studies)—to construct a prior GRN [24]. Building upon this foundation, metaTF further incorporates a de novo GRN constructed from scRNA-seq data using information-theoretic algorithms, enabling precise capture of cell-type-specific regulatory events and thereby enhancing the accuracy of both GRNs and TFA inference. Our ablation analyses of metaTF revealed that removal of either the prior GRNs or the de novo GRNs significantly compromised predictive accuracy, demonstrating the irreplaceable and synergistic roles of static biological knowledge and dynamic transcriptional features in TFA inference.

pySCENIC (and its R-language predecessor SCENIC) is among the earliest published TFA inference methods in this field and has consequently become the most widely adopted [14, 15]. pySCENIC employs a gradient boosting machine-based algorithm, GRNboost2 (previously GENIE3) [53], for GRN construction, and subsequently filters the GRNs using TFBS information within promoter regions. Our benchmarking results showed that pySCENIC achieved excellent predictive accuracy, ranking second among the evaluated methods; however, its overall TF coverage was relatively limited. Another notable finding was that despite employing fundamentally different GRN construction strategies, pySCENIC and metaTF exhibited a high degree of correlation in prediction accuracy across individual TFs.

As a method exclusively reliant on prior GRNs, decoupleR also ranked among the top performers in both TF coverage and predictive accuracy, attributable to the high-quality regulatory information encompassed in its built-in CollecTRI database [11, 12]. Since the GRN constructed by decoupleR is independent of specific scRNA-seq datasets, it demonstrates reduced sensitivity to data-specific factors such as TF perturbation methods and TF DBD class preference, thereby exhibiting greater robustness across diverse application scenarios. Another distinctive feature of decoupleR lies in its systematic implementation of 12 TFA scoring algorithms. We found that the single TFA scoring algorithm ULM achieved predictive performance comparable to more complex algorithms such as Consensus, while substantially reducing runtime and memory overhead, conferring a marked computational efficiency advantage. Notably, migrating ULM to other TFA inference methods, including metaTF and pySCENIC, also enhanced the predictive accuracy of these methods.

For end-users, method selection should be tailored to specific experimental scenarios. When prior GRN information is available and computational resources are sufficient, we preferentially recommend metaTF given its superior overall performance. Moreover, because metaTF achieves high correlation in performance with pySCENIC across TFs while providing broader TF coverage, it represents a promising alternative to pySCENIC. Conversely, the widely used pySCENIC remains a strong choice, as it does not rely on prior GRN information, achieves the second-best accuracy, and requires fewer computational resources than metaTF. In addition, decoupleR with ULM can serve as a valuable complement to both metaTF and pySCENIC, as it is robust across perturbed cell number, perturbation methods, and TF classes, shows relatively low correlation with data-driven approaches, and has the lowest computational cost.

Our findings also offer important insights for the optimization and development of future TFA inference methods. We revealed that the enrichment of reconstructed regulons among DEGs is a critical determinant of TFA prediction accuracy, underscoring the essential value of data-driven information. Concurrently, the choice of prior GRNs also exerted a significant impact on inference outcomes, highlighting the importance of prior knowledge. Therefore, integrative GRN strategies hold substantial promise for future research. Notably, however, our analysis showed that among the currently available integrative GRN-based methods, only metaTF achieved optimal performance, whereas other integration approaches performed even worse than methods based solely on prior GRNs or de novo GRNs, indicating considerable room for improvement. Regarding prior GRNs, although CollecTRI may be the optimal choice for decoupleR, it is not necessarily the best option for other integrative GRN-based methods. Finally, in terms of scoring strategies, the improved performance of ULM across multiple TFA inference methods suggests its potential as a general TFA scoring algorithm applicable to diverse GRN types.

Our study has several limitations. First, the tested TFs account for only 9.5% (135/1423) of the total human TF repertoire, representing merely a subset that remains to be complemented by future, more extensive Perturb-seq studies. Second, the benchmark datasets exhibit an imbalanced design, with the 174 TF perturbations comprising 102 activating and 72 repressive perturbations. Especially, among the 17 benchmark datasets, the largest dataset (GSE133344) encompasses 41 TF-activating perturbations, whereas the smallest dataset contains only one repressive perturbation. Third, the Perturb-seq datasets employed herein primarily rely on genetic-level perturbations, whereby TF perturbation effects are manifested in the altered expression of the TF’s own mRNA. For post-transcriptional perturbations (e.g. small-molecule interventions), comprehensive evaluation remains challenging due to constraints in experimental technologies and the scarcity of large-scale datasets. Fourth, current Perturb-seq experiments have been conducted predominantly in vitro using cell lines and may therefore be insufficient to fully capture the diversity of cellular environments; accordingly, the inference accuracy of TFA methods for complex in vivo environments awaits validation. Fifth, our analysis focused predominantly on single-TF perturbation effects and did not encompass scenarios involving cooperative regulation by multiple TFs. Furthermore, existing Perturb-seq studies have primarily employed scRNA-seq, and benchmark datasets for emerging single-cell ATAC-seq and multi-omics sequencing-based approaches remain lacking [75]. Future research should extend TFA inference evaluation to a broader spectrum of TFs and more complex biological contexts. Although foundation models were not included in this analysis due to their currently limited practical use and ongoing controversies regarding perturbation effects, advanced foundation models or virtual cells that integrate observational and perturbation data and are specifically designed for TFA inference are also expected to hold great promise in this field [76].
