# A transcription factor regulatory atlas for activity inference and perturbation prediction



DOI: 10.1093/nar/gkag897

## Abstract

AbstractInferring transcription factor (TF) activity from transcriptomes and predicting transcriptome-wide responses to TF perturbations remain challenging, in part because available TF–mRNA resources often face a trade-off between precision and coverage and typically lack signed regulatory information. Here, we present TFActProfiler, a TF–mRNA resource and computational framework that learns signed, quantitative TF–mRNA regulatory coefficients by integrating heterogeneous prior evidence (ChIP-based, motif-based, and curated TF–mRNA annotations) with large-scale bulk and single-cell RNA-seq atlases. TFActProfiler contains 2 606 176 signed TF–mRNA interactions and improves TF activity inference in TF knockdown benchmarks relative to widely used regulon resources while retaining broad TF and target coverage. In addition, because the same learned regulatory coefficients can be used to model downstream transcriptional effects, TFActProfiler enables prediction of transcriptome-wide gene expression responses to TF knockdown without training on task-matched perturbation data. When perturbation datasets are available, TFActProfiler can be further refined to achieve performance comparable to state-of-the-art machine-learning baselines. By providing a direction-aware representation of TF–mRNA regulation for both activity inference and perturbation-response modeling, TFActProfiler supports systematic dissection of gene regulatory programs across diverse cellular contexts.

## Introduction

Deciphering how transcription factors (TFs) regulate gene expression has substantial translational significance beyond basic insights into cellular identity. Identifying TFs that are aberrantly activated or repressed in specific diseases can reveal mechanisms and therapeutic targets, while quantifying interindividual variability in TF activity can yield biomarkers and enable disease subtyping [1–4]. Although RNA-seq profiles thousands of transcripts across diverse contexts, expression patterns alone offer limited insight into the regulatory architecture that generates these patterns. Large-scale perturbation assays, such as Perturb-seq [5–7], have advanced our understanding of gene expression regulation, yet comprehensive TF perturbation in individual experiments remains impractical. These experimental constraints have motivated the development of computational approaches that infer TF activity from transcriptomes and predict the effects of TF perturbations [3, 8–14].

Despite decades of efforts in this field [3, 4, 8, 15–22], three challenges persist. First, achieving both wide coverage and high precision in TF activity inference is difficult: methods maximizing coverage using co-expression or ChIP data suffer from indirect associations and non-functional binding [3, 8, 23], whereas curated regulon resources provide higher precision at the cost of limited coverage [3, 8]. Second, most TF activity inference approaches lack uncertainty estimates despite heterogeneous and noisy evidence for TF–mRNA coefficients. Third, models for predicting transcriptional responses to TF perturbations, including “foundation models” trained on large omics data [24, 25] and regression-based models [26, 27], often require substantial task-specific training data and can sometimes fail to outperform simple baselines [9, 28–32], limiting their utility in common scenarios, such as small case-control bulk RNA-seq studies without matched perturbation datasets. These limitations underscore the need for approaches that combine wide coverage with accuracy, interpretability, and applicability when matched perturbation data are unavailable.

Here, we present TFActProfiler, an integrated resource and computational framework that aims to address these challenges. This framework combines broad TF–mRNA knowledge bases with large-scale RNA-seq atlases to learn signed, quantitative TF–mRNA coefficients. Across benchmarks of TF knockdowns (KD) in multiple human cell types, TFActProfiler outperforms existing TF activity inference methods while maintaining broad coverage of TFs and mRNAs. Our approach also provides a reliability metric for TF activity inference and enables the prediction of transcriptome responses to TF perturbations without requiring additional training data.


## Materials and methods


### Construction of the TFActProfiler TF–mRNA interaction resource

TFActProfiler was constructed as a resource and computational framework that learns signed TF–mRNA coefficients by integrating TF–mRNA priors with large-scale RNA-seq data. This study focused primarily on human datasets because of the availability and quality of training and validation data. The workflow includes three steps: (i) collection of TF–mRNA priors, (ii) collection of RNA-seq datasets, and (iii) cluster-wise regularized regression to estimate TF–mRNA interactions.

When the same TF–mRNA pair was observed across multiple datasets or clusters, the corresponding coefficients were first normalized within each dataset before aggregation. Specifically, for each dataset, coefficient values were divided by the root mean square of all coefficients in that dataset, so that weights were rescaled relative to the overall magnitude of that dataset. After this scaling step, coefficients corresponding to the same TF–mRNA pair were averaged to obtain a consensus weight. Both a comprehensive global table and organ-specific tables were generated. The global table aggregated coefficients across all datasets, whereas organ-specific tables were restricted to samples annotated to the same tissue or organ system. Importantly, preprocessing heterogeneity across resources (e.g. alignment methods, quantification strategies, filtering, and normalization choices already applied by the host databases) was intentionally preserved. Count matrices were used as provided by each resource, and no cross-dataset harmonization was imposed beyond the within-dataset transformations noted above.

This design reflects the hypothesis that using diverse preprocessing pipelines and biological contexts at scale, analyzing each dataset under its native conditions, and averaging coefficients at the end would recover robust qualitative TF–mRNA coefficients (particularly MoR and relative effect), even if exact quantitative magnitudes were not accurate and identical across datasets. In addition, TF–mRNA coefficients that changed substantially across contexts, such that the same TF appeared to activate a target under some conditions but repress it under others, were not considered suitable as general-purpose resources for TF activity inference. Accordingly, interactions with positive coefficients in some datasets and negative coefficients in others could partially cancel during aggregation, yielding consensus weights closer to zero. Given that the strength and even MoR of TF–mRNA interactions can vary across biological contexts, the resulting database should be interpreted as a heuristic, data-driven approximation for inferring TF activity rather than as a definitive, universal map of transcriptional control.


### Benchmarking of TF activity inference in human data

The benchmark quantified the accuracy with which regulon-based TF activity inference identified the perturbed TF in RNA-seq experiments, following decoupleR (v2.1.1) [34]. For each perturbation with matched controls, enrichment procedures were supplied with a gene-level signed statistic summarizing the perturbation–control contrast, together with a TF–mRNA prior.

Performance was assessed by determining whether the inferred activity of the truly perturbed TF ranked more extremely than those of unperturbed TFs in the expected direction of perturbation. The benchmarking framework consisted of four components, the details of which are explained in the following paragraphs: (i) RNA-seq datasets in which specific TFs were perturbed and matched controls were available, (ii) TF–mRNA prior knowledge used as regulons for inference, (iii) inference algorithms applied to the data and priors, and (iv) performance metrics that were used to quantify the identification of truly perturbed TFs.


### Benchmarking of TF activity inference in mouse data

Because TF–mRNA relationships inferred from human data can be transferred across species after orthology mapping [57], and because CollecTRI aggregates prior knowledge from human and mouse sources, TF activity inference in mouse data was evaluated using two priors: (i) CollecTRI mouse regulons and (ii) an orthology-mapped version of TFActProfiler originally assembled from human TF–mRNA associations. CollecTRI has previously been applied to mouse datasets and reported to achieve state-of-the-art accuracy for TF activity inference, motivating its inclusion as a reference prior [3].

Perturbation RNA-seq datasets were obtained from the KnockTF 2.0 mouse resource [47] (http://www.licpathway.net/KnockTFv2/download.php). For each TF perturbation with matched controls, only experiments in which the targeted TF was downregulated relative to controls (log2 fold change <−0.5) were retained. Gene identifiers were harmonized to protein-coding gene symbols corresponding to GRCm39 using NCBI RefSeq [35]. Mouse CollecTRI regulons were retrieved via decoupleR (v2.1.1) [34]. TFActProfiler regulons were converted from human to mouse symbols using Ensembl BioMart [58].

The inference process was the same as in the human cell benchmarks. For each perturbation, a signed gene-level statistic summarizing the perturbation–control contrast was supplied to decoupleR enrichment procedures together with either the CollecTRI (mouse) prior or the orthologized TFActProfiler prior. The same algorithms, hyperparameters, and evaluation metrics were used to quantify the rank of the truly perturbed TF relative to unperturbed TFs.

The validity of inferred TF activity scores was further evaluated using scTF-seq data [59], a single-cell TF overexpression platform in which doxycycline-inducible lentiviral TF constructs are coupled to unique 3′ barcodes (TF-IDs), allowing simultaneous measurement of transcriptome-wide responses and cell-level TF dosage. In the original scTF-seq framework, TF dose was quantified as the natural log-transformed UMI count of the assigned TF-ID, and TF-ID abundance was validated as a proxy for exogenous TF expression. The dataset comprised single-cell transcriptomes generated across nine experimental batches. For this analysis, log-normalized expression values were used as input for TF activity inference. Cells were analyzed separately for each batch. Within each batch, TF activity scores were estimated at single-cell resolution using the ulm with the specified signed and weighted TF–mRNA networks (CollecTRI and TFActProfiler).

For each perturbed TF within each batch, the inferred activity score of the same TF was extracted and compared with TF dose across cells assigned to that perturbation. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated for each batch–TF combination when the cognate TF activity score was available, and more than five cells were present. The resulting batch-specific correlation coefficients were used to quantify concordance between experimentally induced TF dosage and transcriptome-inferred TF activity.


### Benchmarking of TF activity inference using phosphoproteome data

TF activity is modulated by protein abundance and post-translational modifications, such as phosphorylation. TFActProfiler infers TF–mRNA coefficients from coexpression-based regression. However, since the training cells may include states with both high TF mRNA and enhanced TF function due to phosphorylation, as well as states with low TF mRNA, the working hypothesis was that TF activities estimated from RNA-seq and TFActProfiler would qualitatively reflect the phosphorylation-driven changes in TF activity observed in phosphoproteomics. To test this hypothesis, mRNA-derived TF activity trajectories (TFActProfiler and CollecTRI regulon enrichment) were compared with phosphorylation-derived TF activity trajectories. Other resources, such as ChIP-Atlas and CellOracle (base), were excluded due to their lack of annotated modes of regulation.

TF activity estimates derived from RNA-seq were compared with time-matched phosphoproteome-based activity calls in a time-course infection model of primary human foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs) infected with monkeypox virus (MPXV) [60]. MPXV infection of primary HFFs induces widespread, time-resolved changes across the transcriptome and phosphoproteome. These changes include dynamic phosphorylation events and perturbations in MAPK, HIPPO, and TGF-β signaling [60], providing a biological system for benchmarking TF activity methods. Public multi-omics data from MPXV-infected primary HFFs were reanalyzed. In the original study, HFFs were infected at a multiplicity of infection of 3 and harvested at 0, 6, 12, and 24 h post-infection (hpi).

For each time point, the log2 fold change in mRNA abundance relative to 0 hpi was computed for downstream TF activity scoring. The preprocessed data were downloaded from the previous study [60] (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-51074-6#Sec41). The TFActProfiler and CollecTRI regulons with ulm [34] were applied to the normalized expression matrices to obtain the TF activity scores per sample. Phosphoproteome intensities were normalized across runs and summarized as site-level log2 fold changes (6/12/24 hpi versus 0 hpi). Only phosphosites with directional functional annotations in SIGNOR [50] were retained, i.e. sites explicitly curated as activating or inhibitory for a TF. TFs lacking directional SIGNOR evidence were excluded from phospho-based benchmarking. For each TF, TFActProfiler and CollecTRI-derived trajectories (Δ from 0 hpi) were compared to phosphorylation-informed trajectories at 0, 6, 12, and 24 hpi using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Because transcriptional responses are often delayed relative to upstream signaling events, an additional analysis was performed in which transcriptome-derived TF activity trajectories were shifted by one time point relative to the phosphoproteomic measurements to account for potential temporal offsets.


### Integrating downstream TF activity into ligand–receptor interaction inference

To evaluate whether incorporating TF activities downstream of receptors improves inference of ligand–receptor (LR)-mediated cell–cell interactions, TF activities estimated by TFActProfiler or CollecTRI were integrated into LR scoring. Following prior work [61, 62], single-cell RNA-seq data from adult human testicular cells [63] were used to evaluate the recovery of literature-curated interactions from Sertoli cells (ST) to spermatogonial stem cells (SSC).

Prior-knowledge LR resources were obtained via LIANA [64, 65], including HPMR [66], Guide to Pharmacology [67], ICELLNET [68], CellCall [61], CellPhoneDB [69], EMBRACE [70], CellChatDB [71], ConnectomeDB [72], iTALK [73], LRdB [74], CellTalkDB [75], Cellinker [76], and a consensus collection [64]. Receptor-to-TF relationships were derived from the OmniPath protein–protein interaction network [77], which was accessed via LIANA. For each receptor, the shortest directed paths to candidate TFs were identified, and a net regulatory sign (activation or inhibition) was assigned by propagating edge annotations along the path. The baseline LR interaction strength was computed using LIANA-derived rankings, specifically the LR Mean, as defined in CellPhoneDB [69], and the LR Score, as defined in SingleCellSignalR [74].

For TF activity-informed scoring, the LR strength for each LR pair was multiplied by a downstream TF signal constructed, following the previously described method [61]: TF statistics specific to SSC were sign-adjusted according to the receptor-to-TF path polarity, normalized across TFs using a softmax function, and then used to weight the LR strength. TF activity estimated by the ulm method was summarized for SSC versus all other cell types to capture direction-aware activation in the target population, as previously described (https://decoupler.readthedocs.io/en/latest/notebooks/scell/rna_sc.html). When multiple TFs mapped to a receptor, their contributions were averaged to yield a single receptor-specific weight. Only receptor–TF pairs connected by a finite directed path contributed to the scoring process.

Several downloaded resources lacked literature-curated ST-to-SSC interactions and were therefore supplemented with these interactions to enable evaluation. Larger catalogs (e.g. consensus collections) entail a heavier multiple-hypothesis burden, which can result in lower apparent accuracy. This effect was expected and was not interpreted as evidence that any given resource was inferior. To isolate the contribution of downstream TF activity, comparisons were restricted to the LR Mean (CellPhoneDB) and LR Score (SingleCellSignalR) frameworks, rather than introducing alternative machine-learning models [62]. Performance was quantified against literature-curated labels using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and areas under the ROC curve (AUCs) were computed and compared between methods. TF activity-informed rankings were compared with baseline LR rankings within the same LR-scoring framework to isolate the effect of incorporating downstream TF activity on the recovery of LR-mediated ST-to-SSC interactions.


### Case study 1: association between TFActProfiler-derived TF activity and cancer recurrence

As an exploratory application of TFActProfiler, previously reported bulk RNA-seq data from 157 treatment-naïve individuals with clear-cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) [4] were analyzed. As previously described, tumor specimens were obtained from the Vanderbilt tissue bank, and postoperative follow-up for disease recurrence was available. TF activities were inferred from the bulk RNA-seq profiles using TFActProfiler with the ulm method, which has been reported to perform favorably for TF activity inference [3, 34]. The clinical endpoint was time to recurrence, as defined in the original study and provided in the public dataset (https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/nc9bc8dn4m/1).

Associations between TF activity and recurrence risk were evaluated using univariate Cox proportional hazards models (lifelines v0.30.0) fitted separately for each TF after z-standardizing activity values across patients. TFs with zero variance were excluded. For visualization, patients were divided into “High (≥median)” and “Low (<median)” groups. Kaplan–Meier curves with 95% confidence bands were generated for the groups using lifelines’ KaplanMeierFitter (v0.30.0). Log-rank P-values were not computed since these curves were only used for visualization.


### Case study 2: detection of lineage-associated TF activity patterns using TFActProfiler-derived TF activity

To evaluate the recovery of regulatory signals from sparsely expressed TFs, a peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) single-cell RNA-seq dataset distributed with decoupleR (v2.1.1) [34] was analyzed. The cells were assigned to major immune populations using the provided annotations, with an emphasis on B cells and natural killer (NK) cells. A previous study reported that lineage-defining TFs, such as PAX5 in B cells and EOMES in NK cells, show detectable mRNA in ~10% or fewer cells within their respective lineages, whereas TF activity inferred from regulatory information is elevated across the relevant populations [3]. The present analysis evaluated whether TFActProfiler reproduces this pattern.

For each cell, TFActProfiler computed TF activity scores for PAX5 and EOMES using the ulm method. For each TF, cells were partitioned into the target lineage versus all other populations (PAX5: B cells versus non-B cells; EOMES: NK cells versus non-NK cells). Differences in TF activity between groups were assessed using two-sided Mann–Whitney U-tests. In line with the previous study [3], statistical testing of TF gene expression was not conducted for PAX5 in B cells or EOMES in NK cells because expression was detected in fewer than 10% of cells within their respective lineages.


### Case study 3: detection of a regulator of human adipogenesis using TFActProfiler-derived TF activity

A published human adipogenesis single-nuclei RNA-seq (snRNA-seq) dataset [78] was used. This dataset was generated from paired white and brown preadipocytes isolated from the neck region. In the original study, stromal vascular fraction cells derived from subcutaneous/subplatysmal neck fat and deep neck fat depots were immortalized by hTERT overexpression and differentiated in vitro under controlled adipogenic conditions. snRNA-seq profiling was performed across a 20-day differentiation time course at days 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20. Nuclei were isolated from differentiating cells and processed using the 10× Genomics Chromium platform with v3 chemistry, followed by sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq instrument. After quality control, the published dataset comprised 25 339 high-quality nuclei from white adipogenesis. In the original study, empty droplets and doublets were removed using CellBender and Scrublet/DoubletDetection, respectively, and integrated analyses were performed using scVI-tools and Seurat. The data were downloaded via cellxgene (https://cellxgene.cziscience.com/collections/b1912131-de29-4f13-b528-164b45d69cfd).

The dataset was used to assess whether TFActProfiler-derived TF activity profiles could recover a TF with established functional relevance in adipogenesis. TRPS1 was selected as a representative factor because the source study identified it as an early differentiation-associated TF and further demonstrated that TRPS1 KD in immortalized white preadipocytes impaired white adipogenesis. Using this published adipogenesis transcriptomic dataset as input, TF activity profiles were calculated and the temporal dynamics of TRPS1 activity during differentiation were examined. This analysis was designed to test whether TFActProfiler with ulm could predict the early activation pattern of a functionally validated adipogenic regulator from transcriptomic data alone.


### Case study 4: detection of a regulator of the mouse hypometabolic state using TFActProfiler-derived TF activity

Published mouse torpor transcriptomic data were obtained from a previous study of fasting-induced torpor in C57BL/6J mice [79]. In that study, soleus muscles were collected under torpor-related conditions, including pre-torpor and torpor, and transcriptome profiling was performed using non-amplified non-tagging Illumina cap analysis of gene expression. CAGE libraries were prepared from total RNA, sequenced on Illumina platforms, and mapped to the mouse mm10 genome assembly. Expression values were normalized as tags per million, and differential expression analysis in the original study was performed using edgeR with TMM normalization. The dataset is publicly available under GEO accession GSE117937.

For the present analysis, promoter-level expression profiles from the published dataset were used to compare the pre-torpor and torpor states. An orthology-mapped mouse version of TFActProfiler was applied to infer TF activity from the mouse transcriptomic profiles, and differential TF activity between torpor and pre-torpor was then assessed. Particular attention was given to Atf3, which had previously been implicated in torpor regulation in the original study. In the same study, Atf3 was identified as a torpor-related candidate on the basis of promoter-level transcriptomic analysis, and systemic Atf3 knockout was reported to alter the torpor phenotype. For comparison, TF activity was also inferred using CollecTRI on the same dataset and with the same condition contrast. Statistical significance of the inferred change in Atf3 activity between torpor and pre-torpor was evaluated for each method.

Of note, this reliability metric should be interpreted with caution. By design, it simply quantifies the self-consistency between prior-based TF–mRNA mappings and those estimated from user-provided data. It does not diagnose which assumptions are violated when discrepancies occur (e.g. linearity, correct edge signs, or proportionality between TF activity and transcript abundance). Consequently, it serves as a heuristic, scale-comparable indicator rather than a measure of ground truth accuracy and should be considered alongside orthogonal validation as one piece of evidence.

Three case studies were analyzed. For the single-cell setting, the PBMC dataset distributed with decoupleR (v2.1.1) was analyzed [34, 80]. Megakaryocytes were excluded because only 37 cells were available, which is insufficient for stable coefficient estimation in the linear regression. For the spatial setting, a chronic active multiple sclerosis lesion dataset with Visium spot-level RNA-seq and histological annotations [81] was analyzed. Gray matter spots were excluded because only 129 spots were available. For the bulk setting, the human cancer cell line expression dataset from DepMap (Broad, 2025; DepMap Public 25Q2) [82] was analyzed. The data were downloaded from the DepMap portal website (https://depmap.org/portal). Across all studies, TFActProfiler was compared against CollecTRI, a widely used curated resource with annotated modes of regulation. In addition, a randomized control model was constructed by randomly permuting the TF–mRNA coefficients learned by TFActProfiler while preserving the overall model architecture. Other resources, such as ChIP-Atlas and CellOracle (base), were excluded due to their lack of annotated modes of regulation. Without signed regulatory information and in the presence of high collinearity among predictors, linear regression coefficient estimates can become unstable.

This evaluation matches the intended use in small-n bulk case–control settings, where the objective is to prioritize TF perturbations expected to move a disease transcriptome toward a healthy reference.


### Case study 5: in silico TF prioritization for reversing IPF toward a healthy state

As a case study of TFActProfiler, previously reported bulk RNA-seq data were used to perform an in silico disease-reversal screen in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) [84]. The dataset comprised 95 lung tissue cores obtained from 10 IPF explants and 6 donor controls. Two cores were selected from each of the upper, mid, and lower regions per lung. Processed RNA-seq matrices and metadata were obtained from GEO GSE124685 and used for analysis. To drive in silico disease reversal, the log₂ fold change of IPF versus the healthy controls was used as the perturbation input. For each TF, a reversal scenario was defined by replacing its activity value in IPF samples with its mean value in healthy controls. Each TF was then ranked by the Pearson correlation between the predicted transcriptomic change under this reversal and the observed differential expression profile between healthy and IPF samples.

### Implementation of TFActProfiler

All analyses were conducted in Python and implemented using scikit-learn (v1.7.1), NumPy (v2.2.6), Pandas (v2.3.1), Statsmodels (v0.13.2), and SciPy (v1.15.3). The TFActProfiler Python library was developed and distributed with versioned code and example notebooks (https://github.com/HikaruSugimoto/tfactprofiler). A web application for visualizing the TF–mRNA database and estimating TF activity from transcriptomic data (https://tfact.wpgsa.org/) was developed in Python using Streamlit (https://www.streamlit.io).


## Results


### Construction of TFActProfiler, a signed TF–mRNA regulatory resource

To systematically characterize TF–mRNA regulatory relationships, we first assembled a comprehensive set of candidate TF–mRNA interactions by integrating evidence from ChIP-Atlas [33], motif-based predictions [26], and curated regulatory databases [3]. These resources provide broad candidate TF–mRNA links, but many links are indirect or functionally ambiguous, and most lack information about whether a TF activates or represses its target. To convert these unsigned candidate links into a signed and quantitative regulatory resource, we further refined these candidate interactions by incorporating large-scale RNA-seq atlases and fitting linear regression models to estimate the signed coefficient of each TF on the expression of its downstream target mRNAs (Fig. 1A). We then used the resulting resource to infer TF activity from mRNA expression profiles and to predict transcriptional responses to TF perturbations.

The resulting resource enables TF activity inference, reliability estimation, and prediction of transcriptional responses to perturbations. (B) Database coverage. Bar plots show the number of TFs, TF–mRNA regulatory pairs, and target mRNAs included in each database.

The resulting TFActProfiler resource contains 2017 TFs, 2 606 176 TF–mRNA coefficients, and 18 163 target mRNAs (Fig. 1B). All TF–mRNA coefficients included in the resource were statistically significant (Q < 0.01). According to AnimalTFDB [88], ChIP-Atlas contains 370 cofactors, CellOracle contains 31, and CollecTRI contains 198. Consequently, TFActProfiler contains 398 cofactors, and it is not limited to TFs. Compared with existing signed resources, such as CollecTRI and DoRothEA, TFActProfiler includes substantially more TF–mRNA links while retaining information about the mode of regulation. The distributions of regulatory sign and effect size are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1A and Supplementary Table S1 (https://zenodo.org/records/20779282). Although resources like ChIP-Atlas or CellOracle’s base networks list a greater number of potential associations, some of these links do not correspond to functional regulation and lack information about the mode of regulation. In contrast, TFActProfiler integrates evidence for the mode and strength of regulation, making it one of the most comprehensive and functionally informative TF–mRNA regulatory databases to date.


### Benchmarking TF activity inference using TF–mRNA regulatory resources in human data

Identifying TFs that are aberrantly activated or repressed in disease can provide insight into pathogenic mechanisms and reveal therapeutic targets, while quantifying interindividual variability in TF activity can facilitate biomarker discovery and improve disease stratification. However, accurate TF activity inference remains challenging because existing approaches often face a trade-off between regulator coverage and prediction accuracy. We therefore examined whether TFActProfiler could improve TF activity inference while maintaining broad coverage, using previously established benchmarking strategies [34].

TF activity inference requires a prior TF–mRNA network, that is, a resource describing candidate TF–mRNA links together with their expected regulatory direction and weight. For example, CollecTRI [3] provides curated signed TF–mRNA relationships based on prior knowledge. Using the decoupleR framework, we combined each prior TF–mRNA network with gene expression data to calculate TF activity scores. We then asked whether these inferred activity scores could correctly prioritize the TF that had actually been knocked down (KD) in RNA-seq perturbation experiments (Fig. 2A). Of note, decoupleR includes multiple TF activity inference methods, allowing us to systematically evaluate which combinations of prior TF–mRNA network and inference method provide the best performance. Across four human cell lines (RPE1, K562, HepG2, and Jurkat) [6, 45], we analyzed a total of 1423 perturbation experiments. The task in each experiment was to prioritize the perturbed TF over all other candidate regulators based on activity scores computed from a given TF–mRNA prior network. We compared TFActProfiler with several widely used prior TF–mRNA networks (e.g. ChIP-Atlas and CollecTRI). Each prior was combined with established decoupleR-derived TF activity inference algorithms (e.g. ulm and zscore).

Among the tested prior-method combinations, TFActProfiler combined with ulm achieved the best overall performance (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S1B). Priors emphasizing breadth without functional filtering (e.g. ChIP-Atlas and CellOracle base networks) showed comparatively lower accuracy. In contrast, subset-based or curated resources (e.g. Literature and CollecTRI) outperformed the broad, unfiltered priors but did not reach the performance of TFActProfiler. To further assess the contribution of TFActProfiler’s quantitative regulatory information, we performed an additional analysis in which we retained exactly the same TF–mRNA pairs but replaced all estimated regulatory coefficients with a uniform value of 1 (TFActProfiler−), analogous to the binary representation used in conventional prior resources such as ChIP-Atlas. Under this setting, performance decreased, indicating that the learned quantitative coefficients contributed beyond the underlying TF–mRNA pair set.

We further evaluated performance using KnockTF, a resource that includes RNA-seq data from TF knockout experiments across a wider range of cell types and cellular contexts beyond the four cell lines described above. In this independent benchmark as well, TFActProfiler consistently outperformed conventional prior-based approaches (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Collectively, these results suggest that TFActProfiler’s regulatory sign and effect estimates enhance the accuracy of TF activity inference while maintaining broad TF and target coverage.


### Human-trained TF–mRNA priors generalize to mouse data

We then investigated whether the TF–mRNA prior constructed from human data could be generalized to mouse data. Previous studies on the evolutionary conservation of gene regulatory systems suggest that transcriptional control logic is sufficiently preserved for cross-species transfer of TF–mRNA relationships [57]. Furthermore, CollecTRI provides regulons for both humans and mice and has been reported to be the best performer for TF activity inference [3]. Therefore, we compared the mouse regulons from CollecTRI with an orthology-mapped TFActProfiler prior obtained by projecting human TF–mRNA associations onto the mouse genome via ortholog information while retaining the sign and weights.

To benchmark cross-species TF activity inference, we applied the same decoupleR-based procedures described above to mouse TF perturbation RNA-seq datasets from KnockTF [47]. As in the human benchmark, we asked whether each prior could correctly prioritize the experimentally perturbed TF from the downstream transcriptional response. Using this benchmark, the orthology-mapped TFActProfiler prior showed better performance in identifying the perturbed TF than the mouse regulons from CollecTRI (Supplementary Fig. S1D).

We next evaluated the validity of the inferred TF activity scores using scTF-seq data [59]. scTF-seq is a single-cell TF overexpression platform in which barcoded TF perturbations are coupled with scRNA-seq, enabling quantification of TF-associated transcriptional responses together with a proxy for TF dose at single-cell resolution. Using these data, we examined the correlation between the experimentally induced degree of TF activation and the TF activity inferred from transcriptomic profiles across 177 perturbation conditions. TF activities estimated using TFActProfiler showed a significantly stronger positive correlation with the measured degree of TF activation than those estimated using CollecTRI (Supplementary Fig. S1E). Collectively, these results suggest that TFActProfiler, despite being trained exclusively on human data, can be transferred effectively to mouse data.


### TFActProfiler captures phosphorylation-driven TF activity dynamics

TF activity is regulated by both TF abundance and post-translational modifications, particularly phosphorylation. TFActProfiler infers TF–mRNA coefficients from coexpression-based regression. However, since the training data may contain cellular states with high levels of both TF mRNA and phosphorylation-enhanced TF function, as well as states with low levels of TF mRNA, we hypothesized that the inferred TF activities from RNA-seq using TFActProfiler would qualitatively track the phosphorylation-driven changes measured by phosphoproteomics.

To test this hypothesis, we analyzed a time-course model of monkeypox virus infection in HFFs [60] (Fig. 2C). This dataset includes matched transcriptomic and phosphoproteomic measurements across the course of infection, enabling direct comparison between TF activity estimated from mRNA expression and TF activity derived from phosphorylation-related signaling changes. We compared TF activity trajectories inferred from RNA-seq using TFActProfiler with those derived from phosphoproteomic measurements. For comparison, we inferred TF activities from the RNA-seq data using CollecTRI, a curated, directionality-aware TF–mRNA regulon resource that annotates whether each TF activates or represses its mRNA targets and has been reported to be the best performer for TF activity inference [3]. For this analysis, we used a curated resource of regulatory phosphosites that annotates whether phosphorylation at each site is expected to increase or decrease the activity of the corresponding TF [50]. Based on this annotation, we defined phosphoproteomics-based TF activity changes from 26 regulatory phosphosite profiles and compared them with TF activity trajectories inferred from RNA-seq data.

TF activity estimates derived from TFActProfiler showed significantly stronger positive correlations with the phosphoproteomics-based TF activity profiles than those obtained using CollecTRI (Fig. 2D and E). Of note, the correlation between TF transcript abundance and TF phosphorylation level was −0.02 (95% CI, −0.32 to 0.29), indicating that there is no significant association between TF mRNA abundance and phosphorylation in this setting. Because transcriptional responses generally lag behind upstream signaling events, we additionally accounted for this temporal offset by shifting the transcriptome-derived TF activity profiles by one time point relative to the phosphoproteomic measurements. Under this time-lag-adjusted analysis, TFActProfiler-derived TF activities again tended to show positive correlations with the phosphorylation-based TF activity dynamics (Supplementary Fig. S1F). These results suggest that, despite being trained solely on transcriptomic covariation, TFActProfiler may capture phosphorylation-linked TF activity changes.


### Incorporating downstream TF activity improves ligand–receptor inference

Previous studies have suggested that integrating the activities of TFs downstream of receptors improves the inference of LR-mediated cell–cell communication [61]. Motivated by this idea, we investigated whether TF activities estimated by TFActProfiler could enhance LR inference from single-cell transcriptomic data (Fig. 2F).

Following prior work [61, 62], scRNA-seq data from adult human testicular cells [63] were used to benchmark the recovery of literature-curated interactions from ST to SSC (Fig. 2G). In this benchmark, the task was to rank candidate ST-to-SSC LR interactions and test whether known literature-curated interactions were prioritized near the top. LR inference depends on two major components: the prior knowledge resource that defines candidate LR pairs (e.g. CellPhoneDB [69] and CellChatDB [71]) and the scoring strategy used to rank candidate interactions (e.g. LR Mean [69] and LR Score [74]). Across the tested prior knowledge resources, augmentation with TF activities estimated by TFActProfiler consistently improved the AUC relative to both (i) LR inference without TF activity information and (ii) LR inference augmented with TF activities estimated from CollecTRI. This improvement was observed for both LR Mean and LR Score-based ranking (Fig. 2H), suggesting the validity of TF activities estimated by TFActProfiler.


### Case study 1: TFActProfiler-derived TF activity is associated with cancer recurrence

As a case study, we modeled time to recurrence in ccRCC using TFActProfiler-derived TF activities inferred from bulk RNA-seq. We analyzed 157 treatment-naïve surgical resections with annotated postoperative recurrence times [4]. Cox proportional hazards models identified several TF activities significantly and positively associated with recurrence risk (Supplementary Fig. S1G and H). These included E2F7, which has been associated with poor prognosis in ccRCC [89, 90], as well as NFYA, NFYB, and IRX5, which are associated with poor outcomes in other cancers [91–93]. Collectively, these findings suggest that TFActProfiler-derived TF activities may provide a prognostic signal.


### Case study 2: TFActProfiler-derived TF activity identifies lineage-associated TF activity patterns

Because TFActProfiler was trained on diverse resources, including bulk RNA-seq data, we hypothesized that it could recover regulatory signals from low-abundance transcripts often overlooked by scRNA-seq. In scRNA-seq datasets of human PBMC, transcripts for the B-cell master regulator PAX5 and the NK-cell factor EOMES are detected in fewer than 10% of B cells and NK cells, respectively [3] (Supplementary Fig. S1I and J). We therefore asked whether TFActProfiler could still recover the expected lineage-associated regulatory activity patterns despite this sparse transcript detection.

Despite the sparse detection of these transcripts, TFActProfiler inferred markedly elevated PAX5 activity in B cells (P = 1.2 × 10−188, D = 3.0) and elevated EOMES activity in NK cells (P = 6.6 × 10−93, D = 3.8) (Supplementary Fig. S1J). These results suggest that TFActProfiler may recover cell-type-specific regulatory programs that may not be apparent from scRNA-seq-based transcript detection alone.


### Case study 3: TFActProfiler-derived TF activity recovers an adipogenic regulator

Adipogenic differentiation is regulated by a coordinated network of TFs, and identifying regulators that act at specific stages of this process is important for understanding adipocyte lineage commitment and maturation. As a case study, we asked whether TFActProfiler could recover TFs with known functional roles in adipogenesis from transcriptome-derived TF activity profiles. For this analysis, we used human snRNA-seq data that provide a high-resolution transcriptional landscape of adipogenic differentiation [78] (Supplementary Fig. S1K). This dataset profiles differentiating cells across multiple stages of a 20-day differentiation time course and was designed to resolve dynamic transcriptional changes associated with adipogenesis.

We focused on TRPS1, as the original study identified it as a TF associated with early adipogenesis and experimentally demonstrated that TRPS1 KD during the early phase of differentiation impairs adipogenesis [78]. Consistent with these observations, TF activity analysis using TFActProfiler showed that TRPS1 activity was significantly increased during the early stage of adipogenesis (P < .01) (Supplementary Fig. S1L), indicating that TFActProfiler recapitulates the temporal activation pattern of a functionally validated adipogenic regulator using transcriptomic data alone.


### Case study 4: TFActProfiler recovers a regulator of the mouse hypometabolic state

A common strategy for identifying TFs involved in a physiological process is to first analyze transcriptomic data to detect TFs whose regulatory programs change across conditions, and then assess their functional importance through perturbation experiments such as knockout. In mice, Atf3 knockout has previously been shown to alter the phenotype of the hypometabolic state known as daily torpor, indicating that Atf3 is functionally involved in regulating this process [79]. We therefore asked whether the orthology-mapped mouse version of TFActProfiler could identify this regulator using transcriptomic data obtained during torpor-related conditions, including pre-torpor and torpor.

Consistent with the reported functional role of Atf3, TFActProfiler inferred a significant increase in Atf3 activity during torpor relative to the pre-torpor state (P < .01). In contrast, Atf3 activity inferred using CollecTRI did not show a significant increase (P = .60) (Supplementary Fig. S1M). Collectively, these results support the utility of TFActProfiler for prioritizing functionally relevant TFs from transcriptomic data in vivo.


### Estimating the reliability of TF activity inference

Inference methods based on prior knowledge of TF–mRNA coefficients inevitably introduce error because the prior information is incomplete, and modeling assumptions can be violated. To assess how well a TF–mRNA prior matches a new dataset, we asked the following question: if the TF–mRNA network structure is fixed and the corresponding coefficients are re-estimated from the dataset of interest, how similar are those dataset-specific coefficients to the original prior coefficients? To quantify this, we re-estimated TF–mRNA coefficients from each dataset under the same sparsity pattern defined by the prior and then calculated an MSE-based score that measures how well the prior-based TF activity mapping agrees with the dataset-specific TF–mRNA structure (Fig. 3A). A lower MSE indicates that the prior is more consistent with the dataset, whereas higher MSE indicates a larger mismatch.

To evaluate this metric, we compared TFActProfiler with CollecTRI, a directionality-aware resource reported to perform well in TF activity inference benchmarks [3], using scRNA-seq data from PBMC [34], spatial transcriptomics data from chronic active multiple sclerosis lesions [81], and bulk RNA-seq data from cancer cell lines [82]. In addition, we constructed a randomized control model by randomly permuting the TF–mRNA coefficients learned by TFActProfiler while preserving the overall model architecture. Across the evaluated cell types and spatial niches, TFActProfiler showed lower MSE than CollecTRI and the randomized control model (Fig. 3B–G and Supplementary Fig. S2). While this MSE-based score is heuristic rather than a direct measure of ground truth accuracy, these results are consistent with our earlier finding that TFActProfiler provides more accurate TF activity estimates than CollecTRI (Fig. 2) and support the practical utility of the MSE score.


### Perturbation prediction using TF–mRNA priors without additional training data

Predicting transcriptomic responses to molecular perturbations sometimes requires additional training on large-scale, single-cell, or perturbation datasets, making such approaches impractical in bulk case-control studies with limited sample sizes. To address this limitation, we developed a training-free (zero-shot) perturbation prediction framework that uses prior TF–mRNA coefficients (Fig. 4A). Previous studies have used CellOracle [26], CPA [94], scGPT [25], and scFoundation [24] for zero-shot prediction [28]. However, these approaches first retrain the models on separate single-cell RNA-seq datasets and then predict perturbation outcomes in bulk RNA-seq experiments. This presents a distinct problem setting, and their performance was not significantly better than random [28]. Based on these findings, we tested only whether zero-shot predictions based on TF–mRNA priors show significant agreement with experimental measurements.

We evaluated this approach using four human cell lines (RPE1, K562, HepG2, and Jurkat) [6, 45]. For each perturbation experiment, we predicted the change in gene expression from the TF–mRNA prior and compared this predicted change with the observed KD-control transcriptomic contrast. Across the four cell lines, predicted responses using TFActProfiler significantly correlated with the experimental measurements (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, TFActProfiler’s predictions were more closely aligned with the experimental measurements than CollecTRI’s predictions were, demonstrating higher correlations and consistently lower MSE (P < .05) (Fig. 4B).


### Case study 5: prioritizing candidate drivers to shift IPF toward a healthy state using TFActProfiler

As a case study of perturbation prediction without additional training, we investigated which TF perturbations could shift IPF toward a healthy transcriptional state. Using bulk RNA-seq data from IPF lungs and healthy controls [84] (Supplementary Fig. S3A), we performed in silico “disease reversal” experiments. For each TF, we set its activity in IPF samples to the mean activity of that TF in healthy controls and computed the resulting predicted change in the transcriptome. Then, we correlated this predicted change with the observed IPF-to-healthy differential expression. Several TFs showed significant positive correlations (Supplementary Fig. S3B), indicating that modulating them would shift IPF transcriptomes toward a healthy expression state. SMAD7 and HMGB2, both of which are associated with fibrosis [95, 96], ranked among the top candidates, supporting the utility of TFActProfiler.


### Perturbation prediction using TF–mRNA priors with additional training data

We next evaluated whether TFActProfiler could improve prediction of transcriptional responses to perturbations that are not observed during model training. In this benchmark, the task is to predict the post-perturbation expression profile for a TF perturbation held out during training. Within this framework, each perturbation must be represented in a form that allows the model to generalize from previously observed perturbations to unseen ones. This is the role of the perturbation embedding: it provides a numerical representation of the perturbed TF that can be used to predict the downstream expression response. Intuitively, if two TF perturbations have similar embeddings, the model can transfer information learned from one perturbation to the other. For this analysis, we used a linear, low-rank model [9], in which TF–mRNA priors (TFActProfiler or CollecTRI) were used to define the perturbation embedding and RNA-seq data from TF KD experiments were used for supervision (Fig. 4C). For comparison, we evaluated scFoundation [24] and scGPT [25] embeddings with the same linear model. Of note, in previous benchmarks [9], the linear model using scFoundation-derived embeddings has been reported to outperform a mean-expression baseline and machine learning models, such as scBERT [85], Geneformer [86], and GEARS [87].

In held-out evaluations of previously unobserved perturbations, TFActProfiler showed higher correlations between predicted and observed perturbation-induced expression changes than CollecTRI, scFoundation, and scGPT (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. S3C). TFActProfiler also showed lower MSE than the other methods (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. S3C).


## Discussion

TFActProfiler integrated comprehensive candidate TF–mRNA links from resources such as ChIP-Atlas and motif-based networks with transcriptomic atlases to assign modes of regulation and quantitative weights to TF–mRNA links. Since binding- or motif-based resources typically lack information about the mode of regulation, learning signed effects directly from transcriptomes reduces sign ambiguity and error propagation in regulon-based scoring. This likely explains the observed improvements in TF activity inference accuracy across perturbation benchmarks, while retaining broad coverage. We consider this an important practical advance, because prior resources in this field have often exhibited a trade-off between comprehensiveness and predictive performance [3, 8], whereas TFActProfiler achieved top or near-top accuracy while maintaining broad TF coverage. Although the regression-based estimation of TF–mRNA coefficients itself is not conceptually new, the significance of TFActProfiler lies in constructing a pre-trained, transferable signed TF–mRNA resource from large-scale integration of prior knowledge and transcriptomic compendia. In this respect, TFActProfiler differs conceptually from CellOracle, which fits regression models to a user’s single-cell training dataset to simulate cell-state transitions after TF perturbation, whereas TFActProfiler was designed for broadly applicable TF activity inference and perturbation-response approximation across datasets, including settings where no training data are available.

A key contribution is the uncertainty-aware readout of TF activity. Given that regulatory priors vary in quality, and assumptions such as linearity and cross-context transferability may not be valid, we defined a simple mean-squared-error-based reliability metric. This metric quantifies the self-consistency between prior-driven inference and data-driven TF–mRNA dependencies. This indicator helps to interpret activity estimates, identifies contexts where priors differ from the data, and provides a practical benchmark for comparing emerging TF–mRNA databases and inference strategies. To our knowledge, this type of prior–data consistency score has not previously been introduced in the context of transcriptome-based TF activity inference.

TFActProfiler shows that large-scale, pre-trained resources can support semi-quantitative prediction of transcriptomic responses to TF perturbations without task-specific retraining. In contrast to recent perturbation-prediction models [24–26] that require additional perturbation or single-cell data for fine-tuning, this framework remains simple, interpretable, and effective in small-sample bulk settings. In addition, the formulation used for zero-shot perturbation-response approximation is, to our knowledge, a new component of the present framework. When additional perturbation measurements are available, TFActProfiler’s performance was comparable to the linear model using scFoundation-derived embeddings [9], supporting the validity of TFActProfiler’s information.

Moreover, the present study is strengthened by a substantially expanded validation framework, including 1 423 TF KD experiments across four human cell lines, independent knockout and activation-based benchmarks, phosphoproteomics-linked TF activity dynamics, and multiple downstream biological applications. We therefore view this work not only as a resource paper, but also as a meaningful benchmark contribution to the TF activity inference field.

The current study has several limitations. Despite the improvements in coverage and accuracy, expanding to rare cell types and diverse physiological contexts requires more data and context-aware transfer strategies. Importantly, the extent to which a TF regulates a given mRNA is highly context-dependent, and the resulting coefficients should not be interpreted as precise mechanistic truths but rather as heuristic approximations for inferring TF activity. Consequently, although the benchmarks show that TFActProfiler is more accurate than CollecTRI, TFActProfiler does not replace CollecTRI, which uniquely aggregates literature-derived evidence. For example, CollecTRI may be more useful when the priority is to trace TF–mRNA relationships back to published literature and assess whether a candidate interaction already has prior experimental support. Users should therefore choose between these resources based on their goals. Although the perturbation predictions are intended to provide directional information, they are merely linear approximations of regulatory dynamics rather than exact forecasts of expression changes. Several other perturbation prediction models have been proposed, including scVIDR [97], scTenifoldKnk [98], Squidiff [99], PerturbNet [100], AttentionPert [101], MichiGAN [102], SCENIC+ [27], GenKI [10], GPerturb [13], and PAIRING [103]. Previous studies have shown that many of these methods primarily rely on single-cell RNA-seq inputs and may be inaccurate for out-of-distribution cell states. Additionally, some methods are limited by their training data. For instance, PAIRING is trained on the LINCS L1000 dataset [104], which restricts the types of perturbations to those represented in the resource and limits gene-level outputs to the 976 targets that were measured. These modality and scope mismatches complicate fair benchmarking against the present framework, which was developed and validated under different assumptions and data constraints. For these reasons, we decided not to conduct strict cross-method comparisons, instead limiting the analysis to demonstrating that perturbation predictions based on the TF–mRNA coefficients in TFActProfiler are significantly correlated with experimental measurements, suggesting that these relationships are reasonably valid. A priority for future work is to evaluate our model against nonlinear methods under matched conditions. Given previous studies indicating that prediction performance is highly context-dependent and that no single method consistently outperforms others in different conditions [28, 105, 106], hybrid protocols pairing the interpretability of linear coefficients with the representational capacity of modern machine learning may be promising. Regarding perturbation prediction in particular, many efforts have already been made, including the Virtual Cell Challenge [14, 107], and more accurate machine-learning approaches are emerging. Accordingly, we refrain from claiming that our method achieves the best predictive performance. The primary contribution of this work lies in the construction of an atlas summarizing TF–mRNA coefficients, and in demonstrating its utility through exemplar applications, which together support the overall validity of the atlas.

A key limitation of TFActProfiler is that the signed TF–mRNA coefficients are inferred from large-scale transcriptomic covariation and therefore should be interpreted as heuristic consensus estimates rather than definitive causal mode-of-regulation annotations. Because TF activity is not always reflected by TF mRNA abundance, and because coefficient signs can be affected by context dependence, multicollinearity, co-regulation, feedback structure, and unobserved confounders, some inferred signs will inevitably be unstable or incorrect in specific biological settings. Accordingly, TFActProfiler is intended as a practical signed prior for TF activity inference and perturbation-response approximation, not as a substitute for dedicated mechanistic GRN inference. As previously described in the literature on TF activity inference and perturbation modeling [3, 26], this approach is not intended to infer a GRN. Many dedicated methods exist for GRN inference [108] and are more appropriate alternatives for that task. Future work should integrate nonlinear or dynamic priors, refine adaptive context selection, and include more detailed benchmarks of perturbation predictions [109].

In conclusion, these contributions establish TFActProfiler as a resource and methodology that unites wide-coverage, uncertainty-aware TF activity inference with perturbation predictions. By combining interpretability, simplicity, and general applicability, TFActProfiler provides a practical benchmark for future method development and a tool for transforming observational omics data into testable hypotheses across diverse biological settings.
